Conserved Water Ledger · Definitions

Terms and practice codes

This page defines, in plain words, each term the ledger uses and the practice codes it assigns. The definitions follow The Fate of Conserved Water, whose appendix gives the same terms with their notation.

Terms

Who and what

Water user
The irrigation district or city utility whose conservation the ledger evaluates.
Practice
The conservation step being evaluated, such as lining a canal, fallowing a field, or installing low-flow fixtures.
Output
What the water user's water produces: crops grown, lawns and trees watered, homes and businesses served. Most practices keep the same output; fallowing and turf removal reduce it.

Water balance

Withdrawal
Water the water user diverts from a river or pumps from a well. In the ledger, it is the total of the paths under Water balance.
Depletion (consumed or lost)
Water that leaves the basin for good: used by plants, evaporated, exported, or sent to a sink. No one downstream can use it again.
Beneficial consumption
Plant use or evaporation that produces the output, such as a crop or a green lawn.
Other consumption
Plant use or evaporation that produces nothing, such as evaporation from bare soil or open canals and water used by weeds.
Drain
A ditch or pipe that carries tailwater away from fields. In the ledger, a drain empties to a sink, so its water is lost to the basin; tailwater that flows back to the river is a return flow instead.
Outflow lost to a sink
Water that leaves usable reach: flow to a salty or unusable aquifer, the ocean, another basin, or an aquifer too deep to pump. It counts as depletion.
Return flow
Water that comes back to a usable river or connected aquifer after use: canal seepage, deep percolation, tailwater, leaks, or treated effluent. People, wells, and streams downstream often depend on it.
Quick and slow returns
Quick returns, such as tailwater or effluent, reach the river within the month. Slow returns, such as canal seepage and deep percolation, travel through the aquifer and arrive months later.
Average delay of slow returns
How long slow returns take, on average, to reach the river. A longer delay moves a loss of return flow later into the year.

The three steps

Before
The water user as it is now, without the practice.
After practice
The practice in place, producing the same output (or less, for fallowing or turf removal), with none of the freed water used again yet. This step measures the saving. It is a what-if, not something a meter would show.
Final
The outcome once the freed water has gone where it goes: protected, used again, transferred, or banked.

Savings

Reported saving
The drop in withdrawal from Before to After practice. It is the figure programs usually report, and the amount of water the practice frees for other uses (the freed water).
Wet saving
The part of the reported saving that would have been consumed or lost. Only this part can become net savings.
Dry saving
The part of the reported saving that would have flowed back to the river or aquifer anyway. Cutting it changes when and where water flows, not how much the basin has.
Change in basin depletion
How much more or less water the basin consumes or loses each year, from Before to Final. Net savings is this change with the sign reversed.
Net savings
The fall in water consumed or lost to the basin each year, from Before to Final. It equals the wet saving minus whatever reuse of the freed water consumes, so it can be below zero. Net savings is a total for the basin over a year; it does not say which places or months gain or lose. It is not the same as Washington's legal term "net water savings," which also requires that existing water rights not be impaired.
Net savings share
Net savings as a share of the reported saving. At one, every unit reported is a unit gained; at zero, none is; below zero, the practice costs the basin water.
Change in withdrawal
The change in water withdrawn from Before to Final, as a meter on the diversion would show it. Only protected water lowers it; water used again, transferred, or banked still passes the meter.

Where the freed water goes

Protected in the source
Freed water left in the river or aquifer under legal protection, such as a trust water right. No one consumes it.
Local reuse
Freed water put back to use by the water user that conserved it: more acres, a fuller supply in short years, or new crops. It is applied with the new practice, which returns little, so most of it is consumed.
Transferred to another user
Freed water that supports a different use, such as new homes or a junior water right. Its effect depends on the share the new user consumes.
Banked underground
Freed water deliberately stored in an aquifer or reservoir for later release. Some evaporates; the rest returns later, like a slow return.

Where and when

Node A
A point on the river just below the diversion, before any return flow comes back.
Node B
A point on the river below every return flow. Over a full year, the change in flow at Node B equals net savings.
Third parties
Other water users, groundwater pumpers, and streams that rely on return flow or on water a practice now consumes.
Flow needed
The flow required at a point and time, such as an instream flow target or the demands of users who rely on flow there. Not yet in the ledger.
Shortfall
How far flow falls below the flow needed. A practice harms no one downstream if it adds to the shortfall at no point and in no month. Not yet in the ledger.

Classification

Practice code
A short label such as C·protected. The letter is the type of practice: how much of the reported saving can become net savings. The word after it is the disposition: where most of the freed water goes.

The continuum behind the types

Every reported saving splits into two parts. The wet part is water that would have been consumed or lost to a sink, so saving it adds water to the basin. The dry part is water that would have flowed back to the river or a connected aquifer anyway, so saving it only changes when and where water flows. The types sit along this split.

All wet: the whole saving can become net savingsAll dry: none of it can
BAC

Type B sits at the wet end and Type C at the dry end. Type A falls in between, because land or lawn taken out of use also stops returning water. Type D is off the scale: it frees little or no water and instead moves water in time or place.

Types

A
Less consumption, less output. The practice uses less water by giving up some output: fewer irrigated acres, less lawn, a lower-water crop. Most of the saving can become net savings, but less than all of it, because the land or lawn taken out of use also stops returning water. A practice that cuts output is Type A whatever else it changes.

Examples: fallowing, deficit irrigation, crop switching, turf removal, outdoor watering limits.

B
Less loss, same output. The practice keeps the same output and mainly cuts water that would have evaporated or drained to a sink. Nearly all of the saving can become net savings. These are the only "pure" efficiency savings.

Examples: soil-evaporation control, removing water-loving brush, lining a canal over a salty or unusable aquifer, reusing effluent that went to a sink.

C
Less return flow. The practice keeps the same output and mainly cuts water that would have flowed back. Little or none of the saving can become net savings. River flow rises between the diversion and the return point early in the season, and baseflow, recharge, and late-season supply downstream fall.

Examples: lining or piping over a connected aquifer, drip or sprinkler conversion where percolation returned to the river, main-line leak repair, indoor retrofits and effluent reuse in inland cities.

D
Re-timing and relocation. The practice frees little or no withdrawal. It moves water to when and where it is needed, and is the natural offset for a Type C practice.

Examples: managed aquifer recharge, aquifer storage and recovery, water banking, conjunctive use, reservoir reoperation.

Dispositions

The type says how much of the saving can become net savings. The disposition says how much of that part does.

How the ledger assigns a code

  • Type D if the practice frees less than half a unit of withdrawal.
  • Otherwise, Type A if the practice cuts beneficial consumption by more than 5% of the freed water.
  • Otherwise, Type B if the wet part is at least as large as the dry part, and Type C if the dry part is larger.
  • The disposition is the destination that takes at least 60% of the freed water, or "mixed" if none does. The paper names four dispositions; "mixed" is the ledger's label for the in-between case.
