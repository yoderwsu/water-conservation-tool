Conserved Water Ledger · Definitions

Practice codes

The ledger labels each scenario with a short code, such as C·protected. The letter is the type of practice: how much of the reported saving is real water for the basin. The word after it is the disposition: where most of the freed water goes. The definitions follow the typology in The Fate of Conserved Water.

The continuum behind the types

Every reported saving splits into two parts. The wet part is water that would have been consumed or lost to a sink, so saving it adds water to the basin. The dry part is water that would have flowed back to the river or a connected aquifer anyway, so saving it only changes when and where water flows. The types sit along this split.

All wet: the whole saving is real waterAll dry: none of it is
BAC

Type B sits at the wet end and Type C at the dry end. Type A falls in between, because retiring a use also stops its return flow. Type D is off the scale: it frees little or no water and instead moves water in time or place.

Types

A
Depletion cut, service cut. The practice uses less water by giving up some of what the water did: fewer irrigated acres, less lawn, a lower-water crop. Most of the saving is real water, but less than all of it, because the retired use's return flow also stops. A practice that cuts service is Type A whatever else it changes.

Examples: fallowing, deficit irrigation, crop switching, turf removal, outdoor watering limits.

B
Depletion cut, service kept. The practice delivers the same service and mainly cuts water that would have evaporated or drained to a sink. Nearly all of the saving is real water. These are the only "pure" efficiency savings.

Examples: soil-evaporation control, removing water-loving brush, lining a canal over a salty or unusable aquifer, reusing effluent that went to a sink.

C
Return-flow cut. The practice delivers the same service and mainly cuts water that would have flowed back. Little or none of the saving is real water. River flow rises between the diversion and the return point early in the season, and baseflow, recharge, and late-season supply downstream fall.

Examples: lining or piping over a connected aquifer, drip or sprinkler conversion where percolation returned to the river, main-line leak repair, indoor retrofits and effluent reuse in inland cities.

D
Re-timing and relocation. The practice frees little or no withdrawal. It moves water to when and where it is needed, and is the natural offset for a Type C practice.

Examples: managed aquifer recharge, aquifer storage and recovery, water banking, conjunctive use, reservoir reoperation.

Dispositions

The type says how much of the saving is real. The disposition says whether that real part survives.

WordWhere most freed water goesEffect on the basin
protectedStays in the river under legal protection.The wet part becomes real water for the basin. This is the intended outcome for Types A and B.
reboundRe-applied by the same user, usually with the new, more consumptive technology.Water consumed in the basin can rise. For Type C, almost any rebound raises it.
transferGoes to another user, such as new housing or a junior right.Depends on how much the new user consumes.
bankedStored in an aquifer or reservoir for later release.Makes the practice partly Type D.

How the ledger assigns a code

  • Type D if the practice frees less than half a unit of withdrawal.
  • Otherwise, Type A if the cut in water consumed by the service is more than 5% of the freed water.
  • Otherwise, Type B if the wet part is at least as large as the dry part, and Type C if the dry part is larger.
  • The disposition is the destination that takes at least 60% of the freed water, or "mixed" if none does. The paper names four dispositions; "mixed" is the ledger's label for the in-between case.
